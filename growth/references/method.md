# The growth method in depth

The frameworks behind each step of the skill, in order. Where two disagree, say so and let the person choose. Sources at the end.

## 1. Must-have before growth

### The survey

Sean Ellis's question: "How would you feel if you could no longer use [the product]?" The answers: very disappointed, somewhat disappointed, not disappointed, and "I no longer use it". The reading is the share who answer very disappointed.

- **The line.** Ellis compared nearly a hundred startups and found that those below 40% very disappointed mostly struggled to grow (as Rahul Vohra reports it, 2018). It is one practitioner's line from his own comparison: a strong direction, to use with judgement.
- **Who to ask.** People who have used the core of the product recently. Superhuman asked people who had used it at least twice in the past two weeks, never the same person twice; Vohra writes that around 40 answers start to give a direction.
- **The denominator.** Decide before counting whether "I no longer use it" answers are in or out, and say which.
- **Its weakness.** Brian Balfour (2013) ranks the survey as the weakest of three signals, because it is quick and prone to false positives. Engagement data is stronger, and the strongest is the three together: growth that isn't trivial, retention, and meaningful use.

### Retention curves

Plot, for each cohort (the customers who started in one week or month), the share still active at each later week. A curve that falls and then flattens shows a group for whom the product keeps working, and the higher it flattens, the healthier the product; a curve that keeps falling toward zero calls for work on the product before growth tactics (Sequoia's data science team, around 2018). Define "active" by an action that means value, never a login alone.

### The must-have engine (Rahul Vohra, Superhuman)

Vohra turned the survey into a loop the product team ran every quarter (First Round Review, 2018):

1. **Ask four questions:** how would you feel if you could no longer use it; what type of people would most benefit from it; what is the main benefit you get; how can we improve it for you.
2. **Segment.** Give each respondent a persona and keep the personas with the most very disappointed answers. Describe that core group in their own words, which Vohra calls the high-expectation customer (after Julie Supan's work). Narrowing to them raised Superhuman's score from 22% to 33% with no product change.
3. **Analyse.** From the very disappointed: what they love. Ignore the not disappointed. From the somewhat disappointed: keep only those whose main benefit matches what the fans love, and read what holds them back.
4. **Split the work.** Roughly half on more of what the fans love, half on removing what holds back the near-fans. Rank each half by cost and impact.
5. **Track it.** Survey new customers continually; follow the score weekly, monthly and quarterly. At Superhuman it became the product team's only key result, and it reached 58% after about three quarters. Vohra warns the score may fall as the product reaches more demanding customers.

## 2. The aha moment and activation

*Hacking Growth* calls the aha moment the point where a customer suddenly sees why the product matters to them; the job is to get as many new customers there as fast as possible.

- **Find it** by comparing customers who stayed with those who left (which early action, how soon?), then ask both groups. Write it as an action and a time window.
- **Check it** before building on it. People who were going to stay anyway do more of everything, so a correlation can point at the wrong action. Help more new customers reach the moment and see whether their retention moves.
- **Map the steps** from first visit to the aha moment, count where people drop, and remove, defer or do for them every step that isn't needed. Ask those who dropped why. Reminders help only when they carry the person toward the value.

## 3. The north star, the growth equation and the inputs

- **The north star**, in Ellis's sense, is the one number that best captures the core value customers get. In a 2024 interview he adds: it should be actionable, able to grow as the company grows (he says it should not be a ratio), and tied to revenue without being revenue.
- **Amplitude's North Star Playbook** (John Cutler and colleagues) adds a checklist (it expresses customer value, leads future results, the team can move it, anyone understands it, it is no vanity number) and three games a product can play: attention, transaction or productivity. A handful of inputs drive the north star, and teams work on those.
- **The growth equation.** *Hacking Growth* asks the team to reduce the business to a basic formula of its levers, so it watches the few numbers that move the result. For a shop: visitors × share who buy × order value × orders per customer per year.
- Choosing, defining and reading these well is the metrics skill's job.

## 4. The stages and the order

Dave McClure's AARRR (2007, "Startup Metrics for Pirates") names the five stages: acquisition, activation, retention, referral, revenue. *Hacking Growth* gives a chapter to each lever:

| Stage | The question | An input to watch | Tests people run |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acquisition | Do the right people arrive? | New customers by channel | The buyers' own words on the page (the book's language-market fit); one channel at a time |
| Activation | Do they reach the aha moment? | Share reaching it in the window | Fewer steps; set-up done for them |
| Retention | Do they come back? | Cohort retention at weeks 1, 4, 8 | Habits around a regular need; winning back people who drifted |
| Referral | Do they bring others? | Invites accepted per customer | Sharing the work itself needs; rewards for both sides |
| Revenue | Do they pay, and more over time? | Share paying; revenue per customer | Prices tested per segment; asking to pay after the value |

The order matters more than the list. In a 2024 interview Ellis says to invest in activation first, then engagement, referral and the revenue model, and acquisition last. Reforge adds that running each stage as its own silo turns one team's win (cheap sign-ups) into another's loss (poor retention).

## 5. Loops, and the four fits

**Loops.** Brian Balfour, Casey Winters, Kevin Kwok and Andrew Chen (Reforge, 2018) argue that funnels describe one step of how products grow, push teams into silos, and run one way, so growth needs constant new input. A loop is a closed system whose output becomes the next input, so it compounds: a customer invites a teammate who invites the next; customers make content that others find in search or AI answers and then make content; revenue pays for ads or sales people who bring revenue. Write each loop as its steps, the step that loses most, and how long one turn takes.

**The four fits** (Brian Balfour, brianbalfour.com). Growth to a large company needs four fits that shape each other and keep changing:

- **Market and product:** a specific problem for a specific group, solved well enough that they pull the product forward.
- **Product and channel:** in Balfour's words, "Products are built to fit with channels. Channels do not mold to products." A product that spreads through invites is built differently from one sold by sales people.
- **Channel and model:** what a customer pays decides which channels you can afford; a low price with costly channels loses money on each customer.
- **Model and market:** the price times the customers you can reach must be big enough for the company you want.

Change one fit and check the other three.

## 6. Networks and the cold start (Andrew Chen, 2021)

When the product gets better as more people in a group use it, growth starts cold: early on, the network is too thin to be useful. Chen's answer is the **atomic network**, the smallest network that can stand on its own: a team of a few people for a chat tool, one train station at rush hour for a ride service, a whole downtown for a payment card. The first one is probably smaller and more specific than you think. Build it, then copy the playbook to the network next door. Win first the smaller group that does most of the work (hosts, sellers, creators), which Chen calls the hard side. His later stages are the tipping point, escape velocity, hitting the ceiling (spam, fraud, saturation) and the moat.

## 7. High-tempo testing

*Hacking Growth*'s cycle runs every week: **analyse** (data and customer feedback on the focus area, shared before the meeting), **ideate** (anyone submits ideas on a standard card), **prioritise** (score Impact, Confidence and Ease from 1 to 10, combined the same way every week), **test** (the top ideas that fit the week), then back to analysis. A team that runs and learns from more tests finds the levers sooner.

- **ICE honestly.** Confidence cites evidence (a test, customer words, data); a hunch scores low. Ease includes engineering time.
- **The team.** A growth lead, someone from product, an engineer, an analyst and a marketer, with design when needed: people who can change the product.
- **The meeting.** Led by the growth lead, same agenda each week: north star and key metrics, tests launched against plan, what finished and what it taught, this week's tests, the idea pipeline.
- **The record.** Every test, result and learning goes into a shared log, so nobody repeats a failure and new members catch up.

## 8. What changed by 2026

- **Product-led growth.** OpenView's Blake Bartlett named product-led growth around 2016: buyers adopt business software the way they sign up for a consumer app, with no sales call first. Activation and the step from free to paid become the core of growth. Benchmarks such as Kyle Poyar's yearly surveys (Growth Unhinged) are quotable only with their date and sample.
- **AI answer engines.** Many buyers now ask ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Perplexity what to use. Aggarwal and colleagues (KDD 2024) showed that content owners can measure and change how visible they are inside AI-written answers. Ethan Smith (Lenny's Podcast, September 2025) describes how products get named there: through the pages, help centres, videos and forum answers the engines read; early products can show up sooner than in classic search; AI-written filler doesn't work. Measure it as a channel: a fixed set of buyer questions, asked monthly, recording who gets named.
- **Must-have moves faster.** Elena Verna, head of growth at Lovable, said in 2025 that in her fast-moving AI category what counts as product/market fit shifts almost weekly (Product School, November 2025). In a fast market, re-run step 1 more often.
- **Channels keep wearing out.** Andrew Chen's 2012 essay argues every channel's response rate falls over time as novelty fades, competitors copy and the audience widens. A new channel works best early; keep testing the next one.

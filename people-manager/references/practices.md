# Management practices, in more depth

The frameworks behind each job in the skill, in our own words, with where each comes from. Sources at the end.

## The manager's output

Andy Grove's rule: a manager's output is the output of the team they run plus the teams they influence. A manager's own time therefore goes furthest on activities that change many people's work at once (a clear goal, a well-run staff meeting), that change one person's work for a long time (a piece of feedback, teaching a skill), or that supply a decision or a fact at the moment others need it. Grove singled out training as one of the most effective things a manager can do. The reverse also holds: a late decision or meddling in detail slows everyone below it.

Julie Zhuo puts the job as improving **purpose** (why the work matters, what success looks like), **people** (the right people, growing) and **process** (how the team works together, which needs more structure as it grows).

## 1:1s

- **The report's meeting.** Grove: the agenda and tone are theirs, and they bring a short outline. The manager listens, asks, and teaches. When they seem finished with a subject, ask one more question; the real issue often comes after.
- **How often:** set by the person's experience with their current work. Someone new to it gets them more often; someone experienced, less. Grove argued for an hour at minimum so that hard topics have room. Camille Fournier suggests weekly to start, then adjusting.
- **What for:** Fournier names two purposes, a human connection and a regular private place to raise anything. Status belongs in writing.
- **Keep a running document** both people add to, with what was agreed and who does what. The manager follows up at the next one.
- **Never cancel; move.** A 1:1 moved twice in a row tells the person where they rank.

Questions to rotate: What is on your mind? What is in your way? What should I start or stop doing? What are you proud of since we last spoke? How is your load?

## How closely to support someone

Grove's **task-relevant maturity**: how experienced a person is with the specific task, which sets how closely to manage them. It belongs to the task: a senior designer new to running client calls is new at that.

| Experience with this task | How to manage |
|---|---|
| New | Say what, when and how. Check in often. Walk through the first ones together. |
| Growing | Talk through their reasoning. Ask before telling. Support and encourage. |
| Experienced | Agree the outcome and the limits. Check the results at agreed points and leave the steps to them. |

Handed-over work still needs follow-up in proportion to the stakes, at the agreed points.

## Delegation levels

Jurgen Appelo (Management 3.0) names seven levels of authority for a decision area:

| Level | In plain words |
|---|---|
| 1 Tell | I decide and tell you. |
| 2 Sell | I decide and explain why, to bring you along. |
| 3 Consult | I ask you, then I decide. |
| 4 Agree | We decide together. |
| 5 Advise | I give my view; you decide. |
| 6 Inquire | You decide; tell me afterwards. |
| 7 Delegate | You decide; I don't need to know. |

Name the level in the delegation brief in plain words. A **delegation board** lists the team's main decision areas (hiring, pricing, tools, design choices) against the levels, so everyone knows who decides what before it matters.

## Feedback

- **Situation-Behavior-Impact** (Center for Creative Leadership): when and where; what was said or done that could be seen or heard, without labels; the effect. CCL adds a fourth step, **intent**: ask what the person was trying to do, which turns it into a conversation. CCL reports that the model lowers the giver's anxiety and the receiver's defensiveness.
- **Lara Hogan's feedback equation:** an observation, its impact (pick the impact the receiver cares about most), then a request or an open question.
- **Radical Candor** (Kim Scott, 2017): care personally and challenge directly, at the same time. Care without challenge is "ruinous empathy"; challenge without care, "obnoxious aggression"; neither, "manipulative insincerity". Scott's order: ask for feedback on yourself first, then give specific, sincere praise, then kind, clear criticism. Her later book, *Radical Respect* (2024), covers bias, prejudice and bullying at work.
- **In practice:** praise often and specifically; criticism soon and in private; one thing at a time; anything hard in a call, never only in writing.

## What makes teams work

**Google's Project Oxygen** studied what its best managers do; the list grew from eight to ten behaviours in 2018. In our words: coaches; gives the team room and does not micromanage; builds an inclusive team and cares about each person's success and well-being; is productive and focused on results; communicates, listens and shares information; supports careers and discusses performance; has a clear vision and strategy for the team; has the technical skill to advise; works well across the company; makes decisions well. Google reports that teams with highly effective managers got better results, were happier and had lower turnover.

**Google's Project Aristotle** found five dynamics of effective teams: **psychological safety** (the most important), dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, and impact. Factors such as colocation, consensus-driven decisions, team size, seniority and workload were not significantly connected with effectiveness at Google.

**Psychological safety**, in Amy Edmondson's words: "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking." In *The Fearless Organization* (2018) she gives leaders three moves: **set the stage** (say why the work matters and that it is uncertain, so speaking up is needed), **invite participation** (admit what you don't know, ask real questions, make a regular place for input), and **respond productively** (thank people for raising things, treat honest failure as learning, act on clear violations). *Right Kind of Wrong* (2023) separates basic, complex and intelligent failures: prevent the first two where you can, and welcome the third.

## Mentor, coach, sponsor

Lara Hogan (*Resilient Management*, 2019) describes four modes: **mentoring** (giving advice from your experience), **coaching** (questions that help the person find their own answer), **sponsoring** (finding stretch work and putting your name behind them), and **feedback**.

Her **manager Voltron**: no one person can meet all of someone's needs. Help each person build a small group (a peer, a mentor outside the team, a coach) and stay the one who gives what only the manager can.

**Core needs** (Paloma Medina's BICEPS, used in Hogan's book): belonging, improvement, choice, equality (fairness), predictability, significance (status). A strong reaction to a small change usually means one of these feels threatened: name which, then address it.

## Team size

Will Larson (*An Elegant Puzzle*, 2019) suggests six to eight people per manager in engineering teams: fewer pulls the manager back into doing the work; more leaves time only for problems.

## Meetings and retros

Grove's two kinds of meeting: **process-oriented** (regular, for sharing what people know: the 1:1, the staff meeting, the operations review) and **mission-oriented** (called to produce one decision; the chair knows what it must produce, keeps it small and sends a written summary of decisions and actions). Cancel a regular meeting whose agenda is empty.

A **retro** in five stages (Esther Derby and Diana Larsen, *Agile Retrospectives*, 2006): set the stage, gather data, generate insights, decide what to do, close. Norm Kerth's Prime Directive frames it: assume everyone did the best they could with what they knew, their skills, the resources and the situation. Remote teams can gather data in writing first, then meet to decide.

## Team health signals

| Signal | Where it shows | The question for the manager |
|---|---|---|
| Past-due tasks piling on one person | tasks past due, by person | "What could you drop or hand over?" |
| Notes asking someone, left open for weeks | open notes, by person | Is the ask unclear, or are they overloaded? |
| One person decides or agrees to everything | who agreed; who wrote the decisions | Which of these could someone else decide? |
| One person is the only one who changes a folder | the folder's history | Who could carry this if they were away? |
| Agent output on record with no reviewer | changes made with an agent | Who answers for this work? |
| Meetings with no written decision | no decision record after the meeting | What did we decide, and who owns it? |
| Someone gone quiet | no changes or notes for weeks while holding tasks | Ask in the 1:1; assume nothing (leave, deep work, work off the record). |
| The same decision argued again | the topic in notes after a decision | Point to the decision on record (decision-making skill). |

Counts describe the record, and much good work leaves no trace there (a call, coaching a colleague, thinking). Use them to ask, never to rank, and never show per-person counts to the team.

## Onboarding and careers

**A new member:** before day one, access, a written welcome with the brief and who is who, and a buddy. First week: one small real task finished, and a first 1:1 on how you will work together. First month: expectations in writing and feedback both ways. End of the first quarter: look at the goals together. In a shared project the newcomer reads the brief and the decisions on record first; an AI can brief them from the record, with sources.

**Career conversations** (Radical Candor, building on Russ Laraway's work): three conversations, separate from performance reviews. Their **life story** (the choices they made and why), their **dreams** (where they want to be at their peak), then a **plan** (what to learn now, from whom, and how the current role can change to get there). Sponsor them toward it.

## Underperformance

- Separate "can't yet" (skills, clarity, load) from conduct (a rule broken). They follow different paths in most organisations' policies.
- Informal first: the expected standard in writing, the gap in situation-behaviour-impact form, support, a review date, a short note shared with the person.
- Formal steps follow the organisation's policy and local law (in the UK, for example, the Acas Code of Practice on disciplinary procedures applies to formal steps toward a warning or dismissal, including for poor performance). Bring in HR or an adviser before them.

## Remote teams and agents (what changed, 2023 to 2026)

- **Written by default.** Remote-first companies such as GitLab write decisions and the conclusions of calls into one shared place, use writing as the starting point, and keep meetings for what writing can't do. Written weekly updates replace status meetings; 1:1s stay live and at a steady time.
- **People now direct AI agents.** Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index describes every worker coming to manage agents. The management rules carry over: brief an agent like a person (outcome, limits, what it may change, check-ins); every agent's work has a named person who answers for it; it is reviewed before it counts as done. Reviewing agent output is real work: count it when you look at someone's load. Feedback is about the person's choices (what they asked for, what they checked), never about the tool.

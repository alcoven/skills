# Common mistakes, and what to do instead

The mistakes teams make most often, in the order work usually meets them. For each: what it looks like, why it hurts, what to do instead, and the skill that goes deeper. Raise one when you see it in a request, a plan or the record, once, with the evidence.

## Choosing what to work on

**1. The request is the requirement.**
Looks like: "Customer X asked for Y, so Y is on the roadmap."
Why it hurts: customers know their problem well and the possible answers poorly; the first idea is rarely the best, and many ideas don't work at all once built (Cagan, *Inspired*).
Instead: ask what they were trying to do when they asked, find others with the same problem, and compare several ways to answer it. Skill: `product-discovery`.

**2. Opinions as evidence.**
Looks like: "Everyone we asked said they'd use it", compliments after a demo, a survey asking what people will do.
Why it hurts: people are kind, and they predict their own behaviour badly. Compliments cost nothing; commitments (time, money, an introduction) show real interest (Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*).
Instead: ask about the last time the problem happened and what they did; count commitments. Skill: `customer-interviews`.

**3. A big bet with no test.**
Looks like: months of building before a real customer sees anything; "we'll learn at launch".
Why it hurts: the most expensive way to learn that an assumption was wrong is to build everything that rests on it (Ries, *The Lean Startup*).
Instead: name the assumptions, test the riskiest with the smallest thing that can prove it wrong, and set the pass line before the test. Skill: `lean-experiments`.

**4. Designing the screen before the job.**
Looks like: wireframes in the first hour; debates about buttons before anyone says who uses it and how often.
Instead: write the job, the moment and how often it comes, then the paths and the edge cases. Skill: `ux-designer`.

## Planning

**5. A list of goals called a strategy.**
Looks like: "Grow revenue 3x, launch in two markets, ship the new app."
Why it hurts: goals say what you want; a strategy says how you will get past the main obstacle and what you will not do. Without the diagnosis, every project looks equally important (Rumelt, *Good Strategy Bad Strategy*).
Instead: diagnose the challenge, choose a guiding policy, and list the few actions that fit together. Skill: `product-strategy`.

**6. Too many priorities.**
Looks like: twelve "top priorities"; every team on four projects; nothing finished this quarter.
Why it hurts: the work switches between items, and nothing reaches customers soon enough to learn from.
Instead: one to three outcomes per quarter, a written list of what waits, and work finished before new work starts. Skill: `product-strategy`.

**7. Output as success.**
Looks like: success means "shipped on time"; roadmaps of features with dates; teams judged on tasks closed.
Why it hurts: shipping is a cost; the value is the change in what customers do. Teams held to output ship things nobody uses (Cagan and Jones; Torres, *Continuous Discovery Habits*).
Instead: give the team an outcome with a measure and let them find the work that moves it. Skills: `product-strategy`, `metrics`.

## Going to market and growing

**8. Positioning by default.**
Looks like: "for everyone"; a list of features without what they make possible; the category the founders first described, kept by habit.
Why it hurts: buyers judge a product against the alternatives they know; if they can't place it, they can't value it (Dunford, *Obviously Awesome*).
Instead: start from the customers who love it most, their real alternatives (including doing nothing), and what you do that those can't. Skill: `positioning`.

**9. Growth before people want it.**
Looks like: paid acquisition, referral schemes and launches while most new people leave within weeks.
Why it hurts: growth spend pours people into a product that can't keep them, and the numbers hide the problem (Ellis and Brown, *Hacking Growth*).
Instead: check that a core of customers would be very disappointed to lose it, learn what they value, then grow. Skill: `growth`.

**10. Plans never checked against reality.**
Looks like: a go-to-market plan written in January and read again in June.
Instead: a short review each week or month: the target, the actual with its date, the gap, what couldn't be counted. Skill: `gtm-review`.

**11. Chasing every competitor launch.**
Looks like: the roadmap changes each time a rival ships; features copied without asking whether customers wanted them.
Why it hurts: you spend your time on their strategy instead of yours, and customers see a follower.
Instead: watch competitors on a set rhythm, ask what each move changes for your customers, and decide on that. Skill: `market-watch`.

## Numbers and decisions

**12. Numbers without dates or definitions.**
Looks like: "Sign-ups are up" with no period, no denominator, no source; one good week read as a trend; total users as the headline.
Why it hurts: vanity numbers only go up and change no decision; a rate without its denominator misleads (Croll and Yoskovitz, *Lean Analytics*).
Instead: rates and cohorts over totals, a date and a source on every number, the decision rule set before looking. Skill: `metrics`.

**13. Decisions nobody wrote down.**
Looks like: the same argument every month; new members reopen settled choices; nobody knows who decides.
Why it hurts: the team pays for the decision again each time, and the reasons are lost when people leave.
Instead: one decider, a short record (what, why, options rejected, who, when, what would make us revisit), and agreement on record. Skill: `decision-making`.

**14. Treating every decision the same.**
Looks like: a week of meetings over a reversible choice, or a one-way choice made in a hallway.
Instead: ask how costly it is to undo. Reversible choices go fast with a small group; irreversible ones get options, a pre-mortem and care (Bezos, 2015 letter to shareholders). Skill: `decision-making`.

## People

**15. Feedback saved for later.**
Looks like: a problem raised for the first time at the review; praise only in general terms.
Why it hurts: the person had months they could have used to fix it, and trust drops when it arrives late (Scott, *Radical Candor*).
Instead: specific feedback soon after the moment (the situation, the behaviour, its effect), and regular 1:1s that belong to the person. Skill: `people-manager`.

**16. The manager does the work instead of growing the team.**
Looks like: the lead writes every important document and approves every decision; the team waits.
Why it hurts: a manager's output is the output of the team (Grove, *High Output Management*); a bottleneck caps it.
Instead: delegate outcomes with the authority to decide, matched to how experienced each person is with that task. Skill: `people-manager`.

# Assumptions and tests

The beliefs a plan rests on, how strong each kind of evidence is, the tests that produce it, and what to change when a belief fails.

## Kinds of assumption

| Kind | The question | Example (Crumb, an invented bakery app) |
|---|---|---|
| Value (Ries) | Do people get value from it, shown by what they do? | People who pre-order once pre-order again within a month. |
| Growth (Ries) | How will new people find it? | Bakeries tell regulars about Crumb at the counter. |
| Desirable (Bland and Osterwalder) | Do they want it? | People near a partner bakery will pre-order for the next morning. |
| Viable | Does it pay? | Bakeries accept a fee on each order. |
| Feasible | Can we build and run it? | Bakeries can set stock aside by 7 a.m. |
| Adaptable (Bland, 2020) | Does it hold up as things around it change? | The plan survives a delivery app adding the same feature. |

Write each as "We believe that [who] will [do what], [how much], [by when]." Bland asks for beliefs that are testable, precise and discrete. A belief that names no behaviour ("people will love it") can't fail, so it can't be tested.

Ries calls value and growth the leap-of-faith assumptions: the two the whole plan depends on. Steve Blank's version of the same idea: a startup is a search for a business model that repeats and scales, and the facts for that search are with customers, outside the building.

## The evidence ladder

This ladder combines Bland and Osterwalder's rules for evidence strength (doing beats saying; costing people more beats costing them less; a real setting beats a lab) with Savoia's "skin in the game".

| Rung | Evidence | Examples | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Our own belief | team conviction, an expert's view, a competitor doing it, market reports | none |
| 1 | What people say they would do | "I'd use that", survey intent, likes, praise for a demo | weak |
| 2 | What people did before | the story of the last time the problem came up; what they pay for today | some |
| 3 | What people do when offered | click, sign up, give an email, book a call, give an hour | medium |
| 4 | What people commit | a deposit, a pre-order, a signed pilot or letter of intent, a payment | strong |
| 5 | What people keep doing | repeat use, retention over weeks, renewal, referrals | strongest |

Three rules:

- **More of the same rung does not climb the ladder.** Strategyzer's scorecard caps evidence from interviews at 2 of 5, however many interviews are run (Luo, 2021).
- **Your own data beats other people's data.** Savoia's point: market sizes, trend reports and a competitor's success are someone else's numbers about someone else's offer. They can suggest a test; they can't replace one.
- **Match the rung to the stakes.** An expensive or hard-to-undo decision needs rung 4 or 5. An early question about whether a problem is real can start at 2. Itamar Gilad's Confidence Meter makes the same point from the other side: opinions count for almost nothing, real use counts most.

## The tests

Cost and time are relative: hours, days or weeks of a small team's effort.

| Test | What it is | Fits when | Rung it can reach | Cost | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Your own data first | Search terms, support tickets, usage logs, sales notes, refunds | The belief is about current customers | 2 to 5 | Hours | Read it for what people did |
| Problem conversations | Ask people about the last time the problem came up | You don't know yet whether the problem is real or painful | 1 to 2 | Days | Use the customer-interviews skill; never pitch |
| Survey | Fixed questions to many people | Sizing something conversations already showed | 1 | Days | Never use it to find out whether people want something |
| Smoke test or landing page (Ries; Savoia's fake door) | A page describing the offer with a sign-up, waitlist or buy button, reached by posts or a small ad spend | A new offer or a new audience | 3; 4 with a price and a deposit | Days | Shows interest from that channel only; an email is not a purchase |
| Fake door in the product | A button or menu item for something not built yet | A new feature for existing customers | 3 | Hours | Say what happens after the click; take it down soon |
| Video (Ries's Dropbox story; Savoia's "YouTube" pretotype) | A short video showing the product working | The product is hard to grasp from words and slow to build | 3 if it drives sign-ups | Days | Measures interest in the story |
| Concierge (Ries's Food on the Table story) | Deliver the value by hand, openly, to a few customers | You need to learn the work and whether it helps | 4 to 5 for those few | Weeks of people's time | Says nothing about the cost of automating it |
| Wizard of Oz, or Mechanical Turk (Ries; Savoia) | It looks automated; people do the work behind it | The value depends on the experience feeling finished | 3 to 5 | Days to set up, then time per use | Disclose who handles personal details |
| Pre-sale, deposit, letter of intent, crowdfunding | Ask for money or a signed commitment before building | Willingness to pay, the price | 4 | Days to weeks | Deliver or refund, and say which when you ask |
| Pinocchio (Savoia) | A model that doesn't work, to hold, carry or click through | Form and fit in someone's day | 1 to 3 | Hours | Shows fit; says nothing about demand |
| Pop-up, or "one-night stand" (Savoia) | Run the whole service for a short time without permanent set-up | A service, a place, an event | 4 to 5 | Days | Tell people it is a trial |
| Infiltrator or relabel (Savoia) | Put the offer into an existing setting (a shop shelf, someone else's marketplace), or relabel an existing product | Demand in a real buying setting | 3 to 4 | Days | Get the setting owner's permission |
| Single-feature product (a zoom-in) | Build one feature fully and nothing else | The value belief passed; you need use over time | 5 | Weeks | Complete for its slice, or a "no" means nothing |
| Spike or technical prototype | Build only the hardest technical part | Feasibility is the risk | Feasibility only | Days | Shows it can be built, never that anyone wants it |
| Channel test | A small spend or effort on one channel, sending people to a smoke page | The growth belief: can this channel reach them at a cost that works? | 3 | Days | Compare cost per sign-up with what a customer is worth |
| A/B test | A random split; one change; one primary measure | Thousands of people pass through and the question is which version works better | 5, for that measure | Weeks | [ab-test-hygiene.md](ab-test-hygiene.md) |

Choosing:

1. The decision's stakes set the rung the evidence must reach.
2. Among tests that reach it, pick the cheapest, then the fastest.
3. Prefer a test that can fail clearly, with a number you set in advance.
4. For an important belief, start light and cheap and move to stronger tests as evidence builds (Bland and Osterwalder; Luo, 2021).

## Persevere, pivot or stop

Ries's innovation accounting has three steps: run an MVP to get a **baseline** (real numbers for where you are), **tune the engine** (changes meant to move those numbers towards the plan), then **pivot or persevere** when tuning stops moving them. Measures for this should be actionable (they show cause and effect), accessible (people can read them) and auditable (each number traces back to people and events): Ries's three As. Ries also suggests measuring runway as the number of pivots a team can still make.

The pivot catalogue (Ries, 2011), with the kind of result that points to each:

| Pivot | What changes | A result that points to it |
|---|---|---|
| Zoom-in | One feature becomes the whole product | People use one part and ignore the rest |
| Zoom-out | The product becomes one feature of something larger | It is too little on its own; people stitch it to other tools |
| Customer segment | Same product, different people | A group you didn't aim at is the one using it |
| Customer need | A different problem for the same people | The people are right; the problem isn't painful enough |
| Platform | From an app to a platform, or back | Others want to build on it, or customers want a finished app |
| Business architecture | Between high margin and low volume (complex sales) and low margin and high volume | The buying process doesn't fit how you sell |
| Value capture | How you earn | People use it but won't pay that way |
| Engine of growth | Which engine you rely on (below) | The current engine stalls while another shows promise |
| Channel | How it reaches people | The product works; the channel can't reach people at a cost that works |
| Technology | Same offer, different technology | A cheaper or better way to deliver the same thing |

A pivot keeps what was learned and changes one part. Changing everything at once is starting over, and the log should say so.

## Engines of growth

Ries names three; a team does best focusing on one at a time.

| Engine | Grows when | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| Sticky | New customers arrive faster than existing ones leave | Rate of new customers against the churn rate |
| Viral | Each customer brings in more than one new one, as a side effect of using it | Viral coefficient above 1 |
| Paid | A customer brings in more over time than it costs to win one | Lifetime value against the cost of winning a customer |

Testing the growth belief belongs here. Once people clearly want the product and the work turns to growing it, the growth skill takes over.

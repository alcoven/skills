# Choosing the segment, the motion and the channels

Steps 1 to 4 of the method in depth. Sources are listed at the end of [plan.md](plan.md).

## 1. The customer count

One row per paying customer (or active user, before anyone pays). Keep it in the plan's folder as `customers.md` or a CSV, with no personal contact details.

| Column | What goes in |
|---|---|
| customer | Organisation, or first name for a person |
| kind | The signs that place them: type, size, role of who bought, what they do |
| started | The month they started |
| pays | Per month or per year, as the team charges |
| stayed | yes, left (month), or still trying |
| first heard | How they first heard: a founder's contact, a customer, a talk, a search, a post, an AI answer, unknown |
| used before | What they did before: a spreadsheet, another product, a person, nothing |
| best fit | yes when they bought fast, use it most, stayed or told others; say which |
| source | Where this row's facts came from: a first email, the sign-up form, the founder, the customer |

Read it three ways, and write each answer with its count:

- **By first heard.** Where most customers came from, and where the best-fit ones came from. When most came through people the founders know, that network is today's channel, and it runs out: the plan needs a channel that reaches strangers like them.
- **By kind.** Which kind holds most of the best-fit customers. That is the first candidate segment.
- **By used before.** The real alternative to beat, which is often a spreadsheet, a person or nothing (the positioning skill works on this).

**When nothing is on record:** fill the table from the founders' memory and first emails in an hour, mark each guessed cell, then call three of the best-fit customers. Ask about the day they started looking ("what was happening?"), what they used before, how they found the product, and who else they know with the same problem. One introduction asked of each customer is also the first referral test.

## 2. Choosing the segment

Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm* (1991; 3rd edition 2014) describes the gap between the early buyers who like new things and the pragmatic majority, who buy what people like them already use. Pragmatists want references from their own kind and a product that does the whole job. Moore defines a market as people who "reference each other when making a buying decision", so wins spread inside one group and stall across unrelated ones. His answer is the beachhead: win one narrow segment, then use it to reach the next, like bowling pins.

Score two to four candidate segments. Give each line 0, 1 or 2, with the evidence and its grade (strong: customers' own words or behaviour in a dated record; medium: the team's report of many conversations; weak: belief; assumed: nothing yet).

| Question | What a 2 looks like |
|---|---|
| A pressing reason to buy | Doing nothing costs them something they feel now: hours each week, lost revenue, a fine, a deadline |
| The whole product | The team can do the whole job for them this month, including setup, the import, the one connection they need, and help |
| They ask each other | An association, groups, events or a newsletter they share; existing customers already refer each other |
| Reachable | The team can name them and reach them this month |
| Small enough to lead | A few dozen customers would make the team known in the group |
| Best customers already there | The customer count shows it |
| Can pay the price | Their budget and who signs fit the price |
| Opens the next one | A neighbouring group that would trust them |

Choose the highest score that the team can reach now; a tie goes to the segment holding more best-fit customers. Write the choice as one line, then the ICP as signs anyone can check before contacting someone ("sports physiotherapy clinics with three to eight physios, where the owner still treats patients"). Name who is left out for now, and when the team will look again.

## 3. The motion

| Motion | Fits when | First proof it works | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Self-serve (product-led) | Low price; one person decides; the value shows in the first session; people search for this kind of product | Strangers sign up, start and pay with no call | The product must explain itself; onboarding is the sales team |
| Founder-led, then sales-led | Higher price; several people decide; buying means changing a habit, a setup or a supplier | Replies, calls, and won deals the founder can describe as steps | Hours per customer; hire only after the founder has sold |
| Partner-led | Another business already sells to the segment and gains by adding the product: an adviser, an agency, a supplier, a marketplace | A partner sends customers without being chased | Partners pass on what sells easily; Janz rarely saw reseller commissions work for software sold to mid-sized companies |
| Community-led | The segment gathers and helps each other: groups, forums, meet-ups | Members bring members; customers answer each other's questions | Slow to start; give before asking; each group's rules on promotion |

Price sets much of this. Christoph Janz's "Five ways to build a $100 million business" (2014) matched price to channel: for customers paying around $1k a year he called inbound marketing the closest thing to a silver bullet, with paid ads and cold calling usually not working at that price; customers paying $100k or more needed field sales. Brian Balfour's "Channel Model Fit" (2017) calls the middle a danger zone: the price is too high for people to buy from a cheap ad, and too low to pay for a salesperson. Teams survive there, often on a patchwork of small channels. A product can mix motions by tier (self-serve for small accounts, a person for large ones); Balfour notes each tier can have its own fit.

At the start the motion is almost always founder-led, whatever comes later: Paul Graham, "Do Things That Don't Scale" (2013), "The most common unscalable thing founders have to do at the start is to recruit users manually." Lenny Rachitsky's study of how fast-growing B2B companies found their first ten customers (2020) found three levers: the founders' own network, going where the customers already are, and press.

## Channel-model fit, with the team's numbers

1. **What a customer brings:** the price per year, less what serving them costs, and how long customers stay (from the count; "unknown" if under a year of data).
2. **What winning one costs through this channel:** money spent plus hours spent (the founders' hours count, at a rate the team sets), divided by customers won. Before a test, estimate it and mark it; after, count it.
3. **Compare.** David Skok's guidelines for subscription software ("SaaS Metrics 2.0"): what a customer brings over their life should be more than three times what it cost to win them, and that cost earned back within about 12 months. He calls these "only guidelines". If a channel can't come near them at the team's price, it fails the check whatever its reply rate.

Early founder hours are partly the cost of learning what buyers need; still, write which channel would fit once the founder stops selling every deal.

## 4. The channels and the Bullseye

Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares (*Traction*, 2015) list 19 channels. A first test for a small team, in each:

| Channel | A cheap first test |
|---|---|
| Targeting blogs | Pitch one story to the newsletters, blogs or podcasts the segment reads |
| Publicity | One news story to the journalists who cover the segment |
| Unconventional PR | One stunt or useful giveaway that the segment would pass around |
| Search engine marketing | A small budget on the exact words the segment types, for two weeks |
| Social and display ads | A small budget aimed at the segment's roles or interests |
| Offline ads | A flyer or a trade magazine ad where the segment gathers |
| SEO (search) | One page answering a question the segment searches (the seo skill) |
| Content marketing | A guide to a problem the segment has, shared where they are |
| Email marketing | A newsletter for people who asked for it, never a bought list |
| Viral marketing | The product invites the next person: a shared page, an invite, a link |
| Engineering as marketing | A free small tool or calculator the segment would use |
| Business development | One partner who already serves the segment |
| Sales | Personal messages and calls ([founder-led-sales.md](founder-led-sales.md)) |
| Affiliate programmes | A share of revenue for people who refer customers |
| Existing platforms | A listing where the segment looks: a marketplace, an app store, a directory; being named in AI answers (the ai-visibility skill) |
| Trade shows | Walk one show and talk to visitors before paying for a stand |
| Offline events | A small workshop or meet-up for the segment |
| Speaking engagements | One talk at an association or meet-up the segment attends |
| Community building | Serve in a group where the segment helps each other |

The Bullseye, in short:

1. **Brainstorm** one realistic idea for every channel, without dismissing any. Teams are biased towards the channels they know.
2. **Rank** them: most promising, possible, long shots.
3. **Prioritise** the three most promising.
4. **Test** each cheaply to answer three questions: roughly what a customer costs through it, how many customers it can reach, and whether they are the customers wanted now.
5. **Focus** on the one that works, until it stops working; then run the Bullseye again. Channels wear out as others crowd in.

Weinberg and Mares also advise splitting time about evenly between building the product and getting traction, and working back from one traction goal to the fewest steps that reach it.

### The test card

```markdown
## Test: [channel], [start] to [end]

- Idea: [exactly what the team does]
- Cost: [money] and [hours a week], by [member]
- Pass line, set [date]: [result] by [end date]
- Record: [table], counted as [rows where column is value]
- Result, read [date]: [counted]; focus | drop | unclear, and why
```

The pass line is the team's own: what result would make them spend the next month on this channel. Set it before the first message; never move it after seeing the numbers.

## Launches

A launch is a push of attention inside an engine: a few days when many people hear at once. Rachitsky's study: "Getting press is rarely the way to get started."

- **Before:** the list of who to tell (customers, the waitlist, the founders' network, the segment's newsletters and groups); the page; a customer's words; who answers questions, and the follow-up written.
- **The day:** one or two places where the segment already is (a community, an association newsletter, a launch site, a talk).
- **After:** a personal note to every sign-up within a day, a follow-up a week later, and where each one came from added to the record. The engine then carries on.

## What changed from 2024 to 2026

- **AI answers are a channel.** G2's survey of 1,076 B2B software buyers (March 2026) found 51% start their research with an AI chatbot more often than with Google, up from 29% in G2's 2025 report. It is a vendor's survey; read it as a direction. Ask the engines the segment's own questions and note who is named (the ai-visibility skill); search still matters (the seo skill).
- **Inboxes tightened.** From February 2024, Gmail asks every sender to authenticate mail (SPF or DKIM: records in the domain's settings proving the mail is the team's) and keep spam complaints below 0.3% in its Postmaster Tools, ideally below 0.1%; senders of 5,000 or more a day to Gmail addresses also need both SPF and DKIM, DMARC, and one-click unsubscribe on marketing mail. Yahoo set the same rules that month and asks for unsubscribes to be honoured within 2 days. Microsoft followed for Outlook.com addresses from May 2025. In November 2025 Google warned it was ramping up enforcement, rejecting mail that breaks the rules. A small team sending personal messages is far below 5,000 a day, but a handful of complaints on a small send is a high rate.
- **Communities.** Buyers ask peers in groups, forums and chats before they ask a vendor. Join the segment's groups as a member, help first, and follow each group's rules on promotion.

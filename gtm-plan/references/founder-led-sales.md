# Founder-led sales

Step 6 of the method. A product needs selling when the team has no customers yet, when the price is above what people pay on impulse, or when more than one person decides. Then a founder sells the first customers by hand, writes down what worked, and only then hands it to others. Pete Kazanjy's *Founding Sales* (2020) is built on that order. Bob Moesta's *Demand-Side Sales 101* (2020, with Greg Engle) adds the stance: selling is helping someone make progress they already want, so the call is mostly about their situation.

Examples here use an invented company, Tallyroom (a tool that sends physiotherapy patients their home exercises and shows the clinic who did them), and its founder.

## The target list

Start warm, then widen:

1. **Introductions** from the best customers ("who else do you know with this problem?") and the founders' own network: former colleagues, investors, friends in the trade.
2. **The segment's own lists:** association member directories, event speaker and exhibitor lists, the groups where the segment talks, public directories.
3. **Signs seen in public:** a post about the problem, a job advert that shows it, a new branch opening.

Never a bought list: it reaches people with no reason to hear from the team, and where the law needs consent, a list bought from someone else rarely carries consent to hear from this team (below).

Keep a few dozen named organisations, each matching the ICP's signs:

| organisation | why it fits | who decides | way in |
|---|---|---|---|
| North Quay Physio | Posted in a sports physio group about patients skipping exercises (link, 12 Oct) | Owner | Group, then email |
| Ridgeway Physio's sister clinic | A customer at Ridgeway Physio offered an introduction | Clinic lead | Introduction |

Contact details stay in the sender's own mail and contacts. The shared list holds organisation, role and first name at most.

## The first message

- **One person, one real reason.** Name the sign that makes it relevant to them, and only one that is true.
- **Who is writing and why,** in the first two lines.
- **Their situation first.** One sentence on what the product does, in customers' words.
- **One easy question.** Ask for their view or a short call; make "no" easy to say.
- **Short:** it reads on a phone without scrolling.
- **Honest:** no fake "Re:", no "following up on our call" when there was none, no invented mutual friend, no false deadline, no posing as a student or a customer. A real name, a postal address and a way to say no.

Write each message; a draft is a start, and the AI never sends it. Three invented examples:

**Introduced**

> Subject: [the customer's first name] suggested I write
>
> Hi [first name], [the customer's first name] at Ridgeway Physio said your clinic has been looking for a better way to send home exercises. I'm [the founder's first name]; I was a physio for nine years and now build Tallyroom, which sends each patient their exercises and shows the clinic who did them. Ridgeway has used it since May.
>
> Would 20 minutes next week help? If the timing is wrong, just say.
>
> [the founder's first name]

**Cold, with a sign**

> Subject: Your post on patients dropping their exercises
>
> Hi [first name], I read your post in the sports physio group about patients who stop their exercises after the second week. I'm [the founder's first name], a former physio. Tallyroom sends the exercises and shows which patients have stopped, so the clinic can call them before the next session.
>
> Is this a problem at North Quay, or have you solved it? Either answer helps me.
>
> [the founder's full name], Tallyroom, [postal address]. Reply "no" and I won't write again.

**After an event**

> Subject: Your question at Thursday's sports physio evening
>
> Hi [first name], you asked the speaker how to tell which patients do their home programme. It's the question we started Tallyroom for. I can show you in 15 minutes how two clinics like yours handle it. Worth a call?

In real messages, end each one like the second: full name, company, postal address and a line to say no.

## The follow-up

- One or two at most, about a week apart, each adding something: an answer to a likely question, a customer's words, a useful link. Never "just bumping this".
- No reply after the last one: add a row `no reply` and stop.
- A "no": thank them, add the row, and never write again from any address.
- After a call, within a day: what they said, what was agreed, and the next step with its date.

## The rules on cold email

A map for a small team sending personal messages; check the rules of any other country, and before any larger send, with someone qualified.

- **United States, CAN-SPAM** (the FTC's guide). It covers business email too, and needs no consent beforehand, but: no false or misleading header or subject line; say the message is an advertisement; give a valid postal address; offer a way to opt out and honour it within 10 business days; and the sender stays responsible for anyone sending on its behalf. The guide gives penalties of up to $53,088 per email.
- **European Union.** The ePrivacy rules leave email to businesses largely to each country. Germany requires prior consent for advertising email even to businesses (§ 7 of its unfair competition law, UWG). France's regulator, the CNIL, allows email to a professional without prior consent when it concerns their job, they were told how their address would be used, and they can object simply and free.
- **United Kingdom (PECR, the ICO's guide).** Companies and other corporate bodies may be emailed without consent; sole traders and some partnerships count as individuals and need consent. Keep a "do not email" list and check new names against it.
- **GDPR, wherever a named person's data is used.** Direct marketing may rest on legitimate interest (Recital 47), but the person must be told who is writing and where their details came from by the first message at the latest (Article 14(3)(b)), and processing for marketing stops when they object (Article 21(3)).
- **The inboxes.** Send from a real person's address on a domain with SPF and DKIM set up, a few a day, each written. The mailbox providers' rules are in [choosing.md](choosing.md).

## The call guide

Before: read their site and the table's rows on them; write one line on why this organisation, and the one thing to learn.

1. **Open (two minutes).** Thank them, say why, agree the time and the plan: "I'd like to hear how you handle this today, then show the part that fits, if any does."
2. **Their situation (most of the call).** The last time the problem came up, as a story; what they use now and what it costs in hours or money; what they tried before. Ask about what happened, not opinions about the future (the customer-interviews skill has these questions).
3. **Why now.** When they first thought about changing, what happened then, and what has stopped them so far.
4. **How they decide.** Who else is involved, what they would need to see, when budget is set, what happens if they do nothing.
5. **Show only what fits,** in their words, and say plainly what the product does not do.
6. **A next step with a date:** a trial with their own case, a call with the other person who decides, a proposal. "Let me think about it" gets a dated check-in.

Notes within the hour, and a row in the pipeline table. Moesta's four forces explain most calls: people switch when the push of their situation and the pull of the new thing outweigh their anxiety about the new and the habit of the present. A call that only praises the product leaves anxiety and habit where they were.

## Objections

| They say | It often means | An honest answer |
|---|---|---|
| "We're fine with what we have." | Habit, or no pressing reason | Ask what would have to happen for them to change. If nothing, thank them; they may be outside the segment |
| "It's too expensive." | Compared with something, or the value is unclear | Ask what they compare it with; go back to what the problem costs them. The price itself is the pricing skill's |
| "Not now." | Real timing, or a polite no | Ask what changes then, and agree a dated check-in |
| "I need to ask someone." | Another person decides | Offer a one-page summary in their words, or a call with that person |
| "What if it doesn't work?" | Fear of a wrong choice | Make the step smaller: a trial on their own case, help with the move, how to leave with their data |
| "Does it do X?" | A piece of the whole product is missing | Answer truthfully. Planned is "not yet", never sold as available; note it for the product team |

Matthew Dixon and Ted McKenna (*The JOLT Effect*, 2022) found that the strongest sellers judge how undecided the buyer is, offer a clear recommendation, limit how many options get explored, and take risk off the table.

## The pipeline table

One row per thing that happened, never one row per organisation changed in place. A log keeps the dates, so the week's counts can be read and the gtm-review skill can tell when the work happened.

`date,organisation,contact,channel,what,by,note`

| Column | What goes in |
|---|---|
| date | The day it happened, YYYY-MM-DD |
| organisation | The organisation's name |
| contact | Role and first name at most: "owner, [first name]" |
| channel | introduction, email, social, event, phone, group |
| what | first message, follow-up, reply, call, trial, proposal, won, lost, no decision, no reply |
| by | The member id of who did it |
| note | One line: what they said, the next step and its date, or why it was lost |

Read the stages from the log: contacted, replied, call held, trial or proposal, then won, lost or no decision. What moves a deal is a next step with a date that the buyer agreed to. A deal with no dated next step has stalled: after two follow-ups and the number of weeks the plan sets, add a `no decision` row. Deals die for a few reasons: no pressing reason, the wrong person, a second decider never met, a missing piece of the whole product, or fear of choosing wrong. Dixon has put the share lost to the customer doing nothing at 40 to 60%, and says more of it comes from buyers worried about a mistake than from buyers happy with what they have.

## The weekly sales hour

The same hour each week: every row of the week added; every open deal with a dated next step; the week's first messages written, as many as the plan sets; and what buyers said that the positioning should hear.

## When to hire the first salesperson

When the founder has won customers more than once by steps they can write down (who to contact, what to say, the call, the usual objections, the next step), and has more good conversations than hours. Kazanjy's second part covers the hand-over: "Taking what you've proven can be done yourself, and getting others to do it, too." A hire made earlier is asked to find what the founder has not.

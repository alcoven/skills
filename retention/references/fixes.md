# The fixes, the way out, an example, and the sources

Each fix answers one reason code from [why-they-leave.md](why-they-leave.md). Pick the one with the most leaving behind it, set its pass line first, and read it by cohort.

## Onboarding to first value (`no-first-value`)

- **Name the first value in the customers' words,** from the stayers' calls: the result they got, such as "the first Monday the reminders went out".
- **Count the steps** from paying to that result, and find where people stall (step data, setup tickets, calls). Remove a step, do it for them, or make it a default.
- **Bring the right person in.** Setup often needs someone other than the buyer: an admin, an accountant, a colleague with a password. Ask for them at sign-up.
- **Help at the stall:** a short setup call for business customers; for consumers, something that works before any setup.
- **Reach out to those who have not got there** by the end of the window found in step 4 of the method.
- Lincoln Murphy defines customer success as customers reaching their desired outcome through their dealings with the company: the result they need, reached in a way that suits them. A small team and a large one may need the same result in different ways.

Measure: the share of each month's new customers who reach the moment within the window.

## Becoming part of a moment that comes back (`faded`)

- The stayers' "normal week" answers show when the product is used. Make it run at that moment: a schedule, a sensible default, a reminder tied to their moment (Monday's list, the month-end close).
- Show the result they got, from their own data, where they will see it.
- One useful nudge at the right moment does more than a series.

## Check-ins for accounts at risk (business customers; `faded`, `champion-left`)

- **Triggers:** the early signals from [measuring.md](measuring.md): the core action down for weeks, the main contact gone or quiet, a failed payment, a ticket left open, a renewal within 60 to 90 days with low use.
- **The check-in:** someone who can help, by call or email, about the customer's goal. No sales pitch.
- **When a champion leaves:** the team's contact for the account meets the new person and offers them a short setup. From the start, set up a second person in every account.
- **Record** the outcome in the account's notes, and whether they stayed.

## A pause or a smaller plan (`need-ended`, some `price-value`)

- A pause with an end date, their data kept, and a reminder before billing resumes.
- A smaller plan that keeps their data and history.
- Offer either beside a cancel button that still works in one step.
- Measure: of pauses, how many resume; of downgrades, how many still pay six months later.

## Annual plans, with care (`price-value`, early leaving)

ChartMogul's 2025 billing report found annual plans keep more customers and revenue at every price band. Part of that gap is who picks annual: customers already committed. An annual plan delays leaving; it adds no value of its own.

- Offer annual after the first value, never as a way round a weak first month.
- Remind before every renewal: the date, the amount, how to cancel.
- Keep the refund policy plain. For plan and price design, use the pricing skill if the project has it.

## Failed payments (`failed-payment`)

Count first: leavers whose last charge failed, and the share of failed charges later paid. Then, in order of effort:

1. **Retries** spread over days; most billing systems offer scheduled or automatic retry timing.
2. **Card updates** through the card networks' updater service, which most billing providers offer.
3. **Before a card expires,** an email with a one-click link to update it.
4. **After a failure,** an email and a notice in the product with that link, in plain words.
5. **A grace period:** access stays while retries run.
6. **Large accounts:** a person gets in touch.

What vendors report, each from its own customers: Paddle says 20 to 40% of churn is typically involuntary, and that sellers with some recovery process typically win back 25 to 30% of these accounts (September 2023). Stripe says nearly a quarter of churn is involuntary, and that businesses using its tools recover 55% of failed payments on average (read 2026-10-22). Recurly's July 2026 benchmarks put failed payments at 1.25 of 3.60 points of average churn. The team's own recovery rate, from its billing tool, is the number to improve.

## Win-back

- **Who:** people who left for a reason since fixed; people whose need ended and may return; failed payments never recovered (they may not know).
- **When:** once something they left over has changed, or their need is back. A message with nothing new is noise.
- **The message:** short and honest. What changed, tied to their reason, a direct link back (with their data, if it was kept), and easy to ignore. One message, perhaps one follow-up. No deadlines invented to rush them, no guilt.
- **Consent:** emailing former customers needs a lawful basis where they live, and always an easy unsubscribe. Never contact anyone who asked not to be.
- **Measure:** how many come back, and whether they stay this time (the "came back" column; a cohort row for returners).

## The way out, done right

- As easy as signing up: online for online sign-ups, a few clicks, no forced call or chat.
- At most one offer (a pause, a smaller plan), on the same screen, the cancel button still in view. A "no" ends it.
- A confirmation email: what ends when, what happens to their data, how to export it.
- Plain copy: "Cancel subscription". Never guilt ("No, I don't want more patients").
- No hidden renewals: remind before renewals and price changes; show when a free trial ends, at sign-up and before it ends.

## The law, by market (as of 22 October 2026; not legal advice)

- **United States, federal.** The FTC's amended Negative Option Rule ("click to cancel"), published 15 November 2024, was vacated in full by the Eighth Circuit on 8 July 2025 (*Custom Communications, Inc. v. FTC*) because the FTC skipped a required preliminary regulatory analysis; its main provisions never took effect. The FTC restarted with an advance notice of proposed rulemaking published 13 March 2026 (comments closed 13 April), asking among other things whether save offers are unfair; no new rule was in force as of October 2026, as far as these sources show. Still in force: the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act (ROSCA, 2010) for online subscriptions: clear terms before billing, express informed consent, and a simple way to cancel. The FTC's 2021 enforcement policy statement reads "simple" as at least as easy as signing up, by the same medium, without save attempts that cause unreasonable delay. Enforcement continues: Amazon agreed to pay $2.5 billion on 25 September 2025 over Prime sign-up and cancellation.
- **California.** AB 2863 amended the Automatic Renewal Law for contracts made, changed or renewed from 1 July 2025: express consent to the renewal terms; online cancellation for online sign-ups, without unnecessary steps; a save offer only with a "click to cancel" link or button shown beside it the whole time; a yearly reminder with the charges and how to cancel; notice of a price change 7 to 30 days before. Roughly 30 states have renewal laws of their own (Jones Day, May 2026).
- **European Union.** From 19 June 2026, Directive (EU) 2023/2673 requires a withdrawal function for online contracts that carry the 14-day right of withdrawal: a clearly labelled button, a confirmation step, and an acknowledgement on a durable medium such as email. Germany has required a cancellation button for online contracts with recurring obligations since 1 July 2022 (section 312k of the Civil Code). The Commission's 2026 work programme planned a Digital Fairness Act for the fourth quarter of 2026; the Parliament's tracker notes problems with cancelling and renewing digital subscriptions, and no proposal had been tabled by its 20 September 2026 update.
- **United Kingdom.** The subscription rules of the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 (key terms before the contract, renewal reminders, cooling-off periods, an easy way out) are expected from spring 2027, according to the government's April 2026 consultation response as reported by Arnold & Porter (May 2026).

Check the rules for each market the team sells in with a lawyer before changing the way out.

## A worked example (invented)

Chairside sends appointment reminders for independent dental practices at £79 a month. Priya, the founder, sells; Tomás builds; Ines does support three days a week. Priya asks: "We lose practices every month. How do we keep them?" Nobody has a retention number.

**The hour.** The billing export since October 2025 gives the month table: 203 practices paying on 1 September, 183 still paying at its end, 17 cancelled, 3 lost to failed cards. "Nine in ten a month" sounds fine. The cohort table does not: of the 86 practices that started from March to June, 52 still paid in month three. Of September's 20 leavers, 13 had paid three months or fewer.

**Who left, and why.** 38 practices left in August and September. Ines codes each from billing, usage, tickets and 21 exit-survey answers. 13 answers said "too expensive", but 9 of those 13 practices never connected their diary, so reminders never went out. The count: `no-first-value` 14, `failed-payment` 9, `need-ended` 4, `switched` 4, `champion-left` 3, `price-value` 2, `unknown` 2.

**Six calls.** Two of three leavers describe the same stall: connecting the diary needed the practice software's admin login, which the receptionist doing setup did not have, and nobody came back to it. All three stayers describe the first Monday the reminders went out and patients confirmed.

**The moment.** Of the 86 practices starting March to June, 49 connected the diary within 14 days, and 44 of them still paid in month three. Of the 37 that did not, 8 did. Written as a hypothesis: "connects the diary within 14 days".

**Speaking up, once.** Priya wants 30% off for anyone who clicks cancel. The AI points out that 2 of 38 left over price once usage was checked, and that a discount would not reach the 14 who never started; it suggests offering the next ten such cancellers a setup call. Priya agrees to drop the discount.

**The fix.** At sign-up, Chairside asks who holds the admin login and books a 20-minute setup call with that person; on day 5, Ines calls any practice not yet connected. Beside it, Tomás turns on retries over two weeks, card updates, and emails before expiry and after a failure, with access kept during retries. The cancel page gets one offer (a pause, for practices closing for a refit) beside a cancel button that works in one step.

**Pass lines, set first.** Diary connected within 14 days: from 49 of 86 (57%) to at least three in four of November's new practices, read 15 December 2026 (with about two dozen starting a month, one month can only show a large change); how many of them still pay in month three, read 1 March 2027. Failed charges paid within 14 days: from 6 of 15 (August and September) to at least two thirds in November, read 1 December. Guardrail: Ines's setup time stays under eight hours a week.

**The habit.** The first Monday of each month, Priya and Ines spend thirty minutes on the month's leaver rows and the two tables; "Customers kept, by month" sits on the project's home. The honest limit, written in the review: 38 leavers were enough to choose the fix, and too few to measure a small change; a before-and-after reading is weaker than a split test, so the March reading decides whether to keep it.

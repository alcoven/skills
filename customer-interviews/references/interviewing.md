# Interviewing: the rules, the questions, the people

How to plan and run an interview that brings back evidence, and how to check one afterwards. Sources at the end of [synthesis.md](synthesis.md).

## The three rules

Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* (2013) names three rules for a conversation that even your mother could not lie to you in:

1. **Talk about their life.** Their problem, their week, their workarounds. Keep your idea out: it invites a judgement, and judgements are polite.
2. **Ask about specifics in the past.** Skip generics and opinions about the future. "When did that last happen?" beats "Do you often...?" and "Would you...?".
3. **Talk less and listen more.** Every minute you explain is a minute they do not tell you something.

Teresa Torres reaches the same place from product discovery: start with "Tell me about the last time you...", because people overestimate their habits when asked what they usually do (producttalk.org, read 2026-10-22). Jakob Nielsen's older rule for usability says the same: "pay attention to what users do, not what they say" (NN/g, 2001).

## Bad data, and what to ask next

Fitzpatrick names three kinds of answer that feel like learning and are not. When you hear one, steer back to facts.

| You hear | It is | Ask next |
|---|---|---|
| "That's a great idea, I love it." | A compliment | "What do you do about it today?" "What have you tried?" |
| "I always...", "I usually...", "We never..." | A generic claim (fluff) | "When was the last time? Walk me through it." |
| "I would definitely use that." "I might pay for it." | A prediction (fluff) | "What are you paying for now to deal with this?" |
| "You should add a calendar view." | An idea | "What would that let you do? How do you do that today?" |
| "It's a real pain." | Emotion without detail | "Tell me about the last time it was a pain. What happened?" |

## Commitment and advancement

An interview with a prospect ends well when they give something up: **time** (a second meeting, a trial with their real data), **reputation** (an introduction to their boss or a peer), or **money** (a deposit, a pre-order, a letter of intent). Fitzpatrick calls moving to a real next step "advancement". "Let me know when it launches" is a polite no. Record what you asked for and what they gave; a synthesis counts these, never compliments.

Ask for a commitment only when there is something real to offer. In early discovery, the commitment can be small: "Could I see the spreadsheet you use?" "Who else on your team should I talk to?"

## Questions: weak and better

| Weak | Why | Better |
|---|---|---|
| "Would you use an app that reminds you of orders?" | Pitch plus prediction | "Tell me about the last order that slipped. What happened?" |
| "How much would you pay for this?" | Prediction | "What do you spend on this now, in money or hours?" |
| "Do you find invoicing frustrating?" | Leading, yes/no | "How did invoicing go last month? Walk me through it." |
| "What features do you want?" | Asks them to design | "What did you try before? What was missing?" |
| "How often do you do X?" | Invites a generic | "When did you last do X? And the time before?" |
| "Don't you think it would be easier if...?" | Plants the answer | "What was easy or hard about it, if anything?" |
| "Why did you choose us?" | Invites a tidy story | "Take me back to when you first thought you needed something. What was going on?" |

NN/g's guide to leading questions adds: do not repeat their words back with your spin, do not name your feature or screen for them, and do not assume a feeling ("when you were struggling...") they did not name (Amy Schade, 2017).

## The story-based interview

Use this for most decisions. One story, dug out in detail, beats ten general answers.

1. **Set the scene.** "Tell me about the last time you [did the thing]. When was it? Where were you?"
2. **Keep them in that one story.** When they drift to "usually", bring them back: "And that time on Tuesday, what happened next?"
3. **Dig out the story.** What happened before? Who else was involved? What did you try first? What did you use? Where did it go wrong? How did it end?
4. **Find the cost.** Time, money, a missed order, an awkward call with a client.
5. **Find what they tried.** Tools, spreadsheets, people, workarounds. A workaround someone built is strong evidence of a real problem.

Torres suggests 20 to 30 minutes per interview and a one-page "interview snapshot" straight afterwards (producttalk.org, 2024); [templates.md](templates.md) has a note in that spirit.

## The switch interview

Use this when the decision is about why people buy, switch or leave: pricing, positioning, onboarding, churn. Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek developed it in their jobs-to-be-done work; Moesta's *Demand-Side Sales 101* (2020) and Christensen and colleagues' *Competing Against Luck* (2016) describe it. Interview someone who recently switched, to you or away from you, and rebuild the timeline like a documentary.

**The timeline** (as Moesta names it in his 2020 talk; older material calls the last stages "consuming"):

| Stage | Ask |
|---|---|
| First thought | "When did you first think you needed something different? What was going on?" |
| Passive looking | "What did you notice or try in the months after, without really searching?" |
| An event that starts active looking | "What happened that made you start looking for real?" |
| Active looking | "Where did you look? What did you compare? Who did you ask?" |
| An event that forces a decision (often a date) | "What made you decide then, and not a month later?" |
| Deciding | "What almost stopped you? What did you give up?" |
| First use, ongoing use | "What did you expect the first week? What happened?" |

**The four forces.** A switch happens when the forces toward the new outweigh the forces holding people in place.

| Force | What it is | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| Push of the situation | What made the old way unbearable | "What was going wrong with how you did it before?" |
| Pull of the new | The better life they pictured | "What did you hope would be different?" |
| Anxiety of the new | Fear it will not work, or will cost too much | "What worried you about switching?" |
| Habit of the present | Comfort and sunk effort in the old way | "What did you like about the old way? What was hard to leave?" |

Listen for vague words ("secure", "easy") and ask what they mean in that story.

## During the interview

- **Two people when you can:** one leads, one takes notes. Steve Portigal's *Interviewing Users* (2nd edition, 2023) covers this and much more.
- **Silence works.** Pause after a question and again after the answer; the useful part often comes second.
- **Follow up.** Ask for an example, then for clarification. Most of the value comes from the follow-ups.
- **One question at a time,** in their words.
- **Keep it casual.** Fitzpatrick's point: a casual conversation about their work gets you more truth and more of their time than a formal "interview".
- **Signal a change of topic** so they know what they said mattered.
- **Leave the guide when the story is better.** The guide is a checklist for the end: did we cover the three big questions?

## Who to talk to

| Who | Why | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Recent switchers to you | They remember why they moved and what almost stopped them | Thank-you bias; ask about the old way |
| People who cancelled or left | They show what failed and what pulled them away | They may avoid conflict; ask about the day they decided |
| People who chose a rival | They show your real alternatives and what won | Hard to reach; offer something for their time |
| People who looked and did nothing | Habit and anxiety in plain view | Often the largest group |
| People with the problem now | The struggle, before anyone's product shapes it | They may not be buyers |
| Friends, family, fans | Easy to reach, good for practising the guide | Never the only source |

"Recently" matters: the further back a decision, the more the story becomes a tidy explanation. If you cannot reach a segment, say so in the synthesis.

## How many

Plan rounds and stop when new interviews add nothing new on your questions (researchers call this saturation). Two studies give a sense of scale, both for fairly similar people and narrow questions: Guest, Bunce and Johnson (2006) found most themes in their data within the first 12 interviews; Hennink and Kaiser's 2022 review of empirical tests found saturation within 9 to 17 interviews. More varied people, or several segments, need more, counted per segment. Interviews show what exists and why; they never show how many people in the market share it.

## Consent and privacy

Before the interview, tell people (the UK Government's service manual lists these): who is doing the research and why; what you will record and how; who will see notes and recordings; how long you keep them; that taking part is voluntary and they can stop or withdraw later; and who to contact. Ask before recording. Keep names and contacts out of shared notes when a code (P3) will do; keep recordings where only the team can open them, and delete them when they are no longer needed. When the person is a child or may be vulnerable, get consent from a responsible adult too, and follow your organisation's rules and the law where you work.

## Recording and notes

Record when they agree; a transcript keeps review and quoting honest. Without one, the note-taker writes quotes word for word in quotation marks and the rest as paraphrase. Write the note the same day, with three kinds of line kept apart: **Did** (what happened), **Said** (opinions, predictions, wishes), **We think** (your interpretation).

## The review checklist

Use it on a transcript or a note, line by line. Each flag gets the line, why it matters and a better question for next time.

| Flag | On whose line | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Pitch | Interviewer | Describing the idea or the product before the end |
| Leading | Interviewer | The answer is in the question; yes/no about a feeling |
| Hypothetical | Interviewer | "Would you...", "If we built...", "How much would you pay..." |
| Generic | Interviewer | "How often do you usually..." |
| Stacked | Interviewer | Two or three questions in one breath |
| Talked over | Interviewer | Filling a silence, finishing their sentence |
| Compliment | Customer | Praise for the idea with no fact behind it |
| Fluff | Customer | "I always", "I would", "I might" |
| Idea | Customer | A feature request with no story under it |
| Evidence | Customer | A specific past event, a cost, a workaround, a switch, a payment |
| Commitment | Customer | Time, introduction or money given, or refused |

Finish with a tally: how many lines are evidence, how many are fluff or compliments, and the two questions that would most improve the next interview. Coach the interviewer; never score them.

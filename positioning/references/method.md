# The positioning method in depth

The method is April Dunford's, from *Obviously Awesome* (2019; second edition 2026) and *Sales Pitch* (2023), with what she and other practitioners have said since. Where she is quoted, the source is in Sources at the end.

## What changed in 2026

The first edition had five components (plus an optional trend) and ten steps: start with your happiest customers, assemble a team, agree on words and drop old baggage, list the alternatives, isolate unique attributes, map them to value themes, find who cares a lot, choose a market frame, layer on a trend with care, capture and share. The 2026 edition keeps five components and cuts the process to five steps: the early steps become pre-work (what you are positioning, who takes part, go-to-market choices) and the last ones an expanded section on the sales pitch, testing it, and messaging. The differentiated value step grew most, since teams got stuck there. This skill follows the 2026 shape.

## Before the exercise

- **What are you positioning?** One product, a product line, an offer, or the company. A suite can share one positioning when the value is the same across segments.
- **Who takes part.** People who sell, build, support and market the product, and someone who can decide. Positioning shapes pricing, the sales motion and the roadmap; a marketing-only draft gets ignored.
- **Go-to-market choices.** Who buys, how they buy (self-serve, sales-led, land and expand), the price band. Dunford names unresolved go-to-market questions as a common reason positioning work stalls.
- **The baggage.** How the product was first described usually comes from where it started. Write it down so the group can choose to keep it or drop it on evidence.
- **Is there enough evidence?** Without customers who love the product, the canvas is a hypothesis (Dunford, 2026). Keep it loose, sell, watch who buys and stays, then narrow. The customer-interviews skill collects the stories; Sean Ellis's "very disappointed" question (the growth skill) shows who would miss the product most, the group Rahul Vohra used at Superhuman to define who to build and sell for.

## Evidence grades

Every canvas line carries its source and a grade.

| Grade | What counts |
|---|---|
| Strong | A customer's own words or behaviour in a dated record: interview notes, a call recording or summary, a win or loss note, usage or churn data |
| Medium | The team's report of many conversations ("sales hear this weekly"), survey answers, reviews |
| Weak | Belief, a founder's memory, competitor websites, analyst reports |
| Assumed | Nothing yet. Say what would confirm it |

## 1. Best-fit customers

- Pick the customers who bought quickly, needed little persuasion, got value soon, stayed, grew, referred others or asked for little discount. Dunford's 2026 advice: study wins more than losses, since wins show what is working.
- Look for what they share: situation, setup, what they used before, the trigger that made them look. Averaging all customers hides the pattern.
- Write their words verbatim with the source. They are the raw material for value and for copy.

## 2. Competitive alternatives

- Ask the best-fit customers, and the people who talk to them, what they would use if the product did not exist. In a 2018 interview Dunford said the answer she hears most often is a spreadsheet.
- The status quo is the first alternative to beat: the customer had the problem before you arrived and is managing somehow. In *Sales Pitch* she treats "do nothing" as the most dangerous competitor; she has estimated that enterprise software loses 20 to 30% of deals to "no decision" (2020). Matthew Dixon and Ted McKenna's *The JOLT Effect* (2022) studies the same stall from the buyer's fear of making a mistake.
- Check every time for: doing nothing; a spreadsheet, document or whiteboard; a person (a hire, an intern, an agency, a favour); a feature inside a tool they already pay for; a general AI assistant; the named products that appear in deals.
- **Cluster** alternatives into approaches ("messages and a spreadsheet", "a shop-builder plug-in", "an industrial suite"). The pitch speaks to approaches, which is easier to follow than ten logos.
- For each approach, write what it does well. Buyers know it, and a fair description earns trust; Dunford's pitch storyline treats the alternatives objectively.
- **Phantom competitors** are products the team worries about that never show up in deals. Positioning against them dilutes the story. Keep them on a watch list and move them in when they appear.

## 3. Differentiated capabilities

- What you have or do that the alternatives lack: features, also delivery, service, expertise, data, partnerships, the business model or price structure.
- Each must be provable: a demo, a customer story, a test.
- It needs to be meaningfully different from the real alternatives; it may exist elsewhere in the market (Dunford, 2026).
- **Product pessimism.** Teams close to the product often undervalue what customers praise. Read the customers' words before deciding a capability is ordinary.

## 4. Differentiated value

- For each capability ask "so what?" from the customer's side: capability, then what it lets them do, then what that is worth (time, money, risk, a result they are measured on). Stop at something they would notice or pay for.
- Group the results into value themes. Dunford finds most products end with fewer than three, and advises one or two specific, defensible themes, ideally one to lead.
- Attach proof to each theme: a customer's words, a number with its source, a demo, a third party. Proof should come from customers like the best-fit ones.
- Send back placeholders. Dunford calls "we save time" a placeholder; ask how much time, for whom, compared with which alternative.

## 5. Who cares a lot

- The characteristics that make a customer care about the lead value: the situation they are in, what they use now, the volume or frequency of the problem, the trigger.
- Signs a seller, an ad or a sign-up form could spot. "Mid-sized companies" can't be acted on; "supplies five or more cafés every morning" can.
- Who you leave out, and why. A narrower group that buys fast beats a broad one that needs convincing.

## 6. Market category

- A category is a shortcut: it tells buyers what to compare you with, which features to expect, who it is for and roughly what it costs. Dunford's example is email and chat, which share features but carry different expectations.
- For each candidate, write those four assumptions and mark which are true of you. Choose the frame that makes your value obvious to the best-fit customers.
- Find candidates in where best-fit customers file you, the categories of the alternatives they named, and adjacent categories whose buyers have the same problem.
- **Styles.** Head-to-head: lead an existing category, which takes a clear advantage on what buyers care about, plus money and speed. Big fish, small pond: lead a sub-segment of an existing category whose buyers have needs the leader doesn't meet; it asks least of a young company's budget. Create a new game: a new category, the hardest and costliest. Dunford notes category creators often lose to later entrants who benefit from the education, and many leaders started in an existing category and redrew it later.
- The category-design school (*Play Bigger*, 2016) argues for creating and owning a category. Weigh that against the cost of teaching it, and buyers' need for a frame they recognise on day one.

## 7. Point of view and trends

- The 2019 edition adds a trend as an optional layer, used with care: it should make existing value feel urgent, and one that doesn't fit makes the product look like it is chasing fashion. She has since said trends matter less than a clear market definition.
- In 2026 Dunford says buyers in a shifting market want a point of view: where things are heading and what to do now, as a path in steps with the value at each, keeping what ships now apart from what is in development. Vague "everything is changing" talk doesn't help.
- The point of view opens the sales pitch as its insight (see copy-review.md).

## AI and the alternatives

- When every competitor claims AI, saying you use it differentiates nothing (Dunford, 2026). Name the outcome AI makes possible for the best-fit customer, and prove it.
- AI has changed many buyers' set of alternatives. "We'd just use a general AI assistant" is a real alternative: list it, describe fairly what it does well, and find where it falls short for these customers in their setting (what they still do by hand around it, what it doesn't hold or connect to, who checks its work). Never claim it can't do what it can.
- Position against what prospects shortlist today, and build for the competitors you expect; Dunford says it is fine to position one way now while building toward another.

## Repositioning

- Triggers and symptoms are in SKILL.md. Early warning comes from customer advisory groups, regular calls between leaders and key accounts, the sales team and win analysis, before losses show (Dunford, 2026).
- Cadence: a set review about every six months. Teams often want new words after a few weeks out of boredom (Dunford, 2026); that is no reason to change.
- Positioning describes where you are now and strategy where you are going, so positioning changes at each stage of the strategy (Dunford, 2021).

## History, and the positioning statement

- Jack Trout wrote about "positioning" in *Industrial Marketing* in 1969; with Al Ries he developed it in *Advertising Age* (1972) and *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981). Their idea, for advertising: a brand holds a place in the prospect's mind relative to competitors.
- Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm* (1991) applied it to technology products: win a beachhead segment of pragmatic buyers, give them the whole product, then expand. His positioning statement fills in the target customer, their dissatisfaction with the current alternative, the category, the key benefit, the alternative you are compared with, and what is different.
- Dunford's critique: the fill-in statement assumes one right answer per blank, while most products could sit in several categories with different competitors and customers. Use the statement as the canvas's summary line, written last.

## Limits

- The method grew from business-to-business technology. For self-serve and consumer products, the homepage, store page and onboarding do the pitch's work, and evidence comes from sign-up answers ("what would you use otherwise?"), reviews and cancellation reasons.
- Positioning can't fix a product with nothing meaningfully different. Dunford: "marketing pixie dust cannot solve all problems" (2018).
- AI tools can draft and check a canvas; Dunford's 2026 view is that they can't replace the team's context and judgement about what is truly different.
